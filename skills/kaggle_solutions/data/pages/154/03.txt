Facebook Recruiting IV: Human or Robot?

Solution Writeup · 2nd place · Jun 10, 2015

Here's a little blurb about how I approached feature generation and cross-validation for this problem:

http://small-yellow-duck.github.io/auction.html

code: https://github.com/small-yellow-duck/facebook_auction

I used sklearn's RandomForestClassifier. The most useful features I identified were:

the median time between a user's bid and that user's previous bid
the mean number of bids a user made per auction
the entropy for how many bids a user placed on each day of the week
the means of the per-auction URL entropy and IP entropy for each user
the maximum number of bids in a 20 min span
the total number of bids placed by the user
the average number of bids a user placed per referring URL
the number of bids placed by the user on each of the three weekdays in the data
the minimum and median times between a user's bid and the previous bid by another user in the same auction.
the fraction of IPs used by a bidder which were also used by another user which was a bot

Cross-validation was a bit tricky: I did CV 100 times on different 80-20 splits. My mean CV score was almost the same (+/- 0.003) as my final leaderboard score.

edit to answer some further questions:

In order to convert the time scale to hours, I made a bids/unit time histogram and looked at the periodicity - bidding volumes cycled with a period of one day. There are pretty graphs and more details on my github page.

A concrete example of the entropy is the IP entropy: N!/(N_{IP1}! N{IP2}!... N{IPn}!). N is the total number of bids and N_{IPn} is the total number of bids placed from the nth IP. (Because the entropy is a large number, I used the log of the entropy.) The entropy is a measure of how both randomly distributed the bids are, how many IPs there are and how many bids there are. A bidder that places all their bids from the same IP has an entropy of N!/N! = 1.

I used the simple average of five RandomForestClassifiers (each with a different value for the initialization parameter, random_state) to generate the predictions.

Thanks to everyone else who shared their approaches! I thought that leaving out the bots with only one bid was a clever idea. And I was surprised to see that people got mileage out of the device type.

Well, that was an unexpected surprise this morning. I was just hoping to stay in the top 10%.
Congratulations to small yellow duck and the other top scorers.

My approach seems similar to a number of the ones posted above. I used scikit-learn GradientBoostingClassifier (thanks to the authors!).

Early on I saw that CV was going to be relatively inaccurate so I ended up choosing 500 resamples of different 2/3 – 1/3 splits. This took the standard error on my CV AUC calculation down to about 0.0007 so that I could reasonably have an idea of whether each feature I tested was making a positive or negative difference. The inaccuracy on a single CV fold and the helpful post by T. Scharf, https://www.kaggle.com/c/facebook-recruiting-iv-human-or-bot/forums/t/14394/a-basic-look-at-lb-scores
suggested that public LB scores were not going to be particularly useful so I only made 3 submissions to avoid any temptation to overfit the public LB - I find this a very difficult thing to do…


Some of my most helpful features were:

- The proportion of each bidder’s bids that were in each day ( I believe there were 9 days of bid data).
- The proportion of each bidder’s bids that were in each of 8 3-hour time slots.
- The mean time of day for the bids of each bidder.
- The total number of bids for each bidder.
- The frequency of each final part of the IP address averaged for each bidder – the first three parts had no positive effect.
- The average proportion of bots associated with each device code. NB – (difficult to put into CV without data leakage – a slightly different data set for each fold!)
- Same as above for each country code.
- The total number of bids made in each auction averaged for each bidder. (i.e. did the bidder tend to bid in popular auctions or less popular ones)
- The maximum number of auctions a bidder has participated in, in one hour.

Hopefully, this gives a flavour of what I was trying. Some of the features are quite hard to put into words!

One final trick that may have made the difference is that I looked at the histogram of the number of bids made by each bot in the training set. There were 5 bots that made just one bid, which looked anomalous to my eye. I wondered if this might not help to predict on a different data set so I submitted an entry which was just my main model but trained on the training set without these five bots. I think this one was the winner, but I’ll confirm when I can see all my submission private scores.

I’ll post in greater detail when I have written my model up. Apologies, as was not expecting to win!

Congratulations to all the winners.

My approach is outlined in this Jupyter notebook.

I used somewhat of a text classification approach. I concatenated the bid entries into one long space-delimited string, and then performed tfidf-vectorization on these text features. The idea was that bots would exhibit certain sequential patterns in their behavior that could be discovered with this type of a text mining approach.

In addition to the text features, I also computed some descriptive statistics (median, max, min, range, deciles) for the time differences between bids, along with counts of unique entries for the columns.

In CV, the numeric features stood for the majority of the auc-gain, but the text-features definitely helped a bit.

Congrats smallyellowduck, and awesome writeup!

I also used the 'everything but the kitchen sink' approach lol.

7862 total features were used. (Most of these are just useless device counts)

Simple stats (mean, std, min, max) were computed over the following features:

* Diversity
  * # unique counties, devices, ips, (countries, devices), (countries, ips), (devices, ips) per auction

* Streaks
  * longest streak of the same device, ip, (device, ip) per auction

* Peak
  * for overlapping time windows:
  * # of bids in a given window
  * # of auctions in a given window
  * # unique auctions in the argmax window

* Count-Vectors
  * count vectors for each unique device, country, merch

* First Last
  * # instances a bidder submitted the first, last bid of an auction

* Consecutive
  * # instances that a bidder has 0, 1, 2, ..., 10 bids in between their bids

* Wavelets
  * 3-level db4 wavelet decomposition for 3 overlapping windowed histograms of # of bids
    * |mean|, std of coefficients
    * differences in |mean|, std of coefficients between windows

* Time Difference
  * time since previous bid

* Position
  * # of bids in windows relative to auction life

Model
=====
Simple average of extra-trees, gradient boosted trees, neural net. For the neural net, I used the top 1000 features as prioritized by the extra-trees classifier.

Other
=====
I always try semi-supervised learning... and it never helps my CV or LB score. =[

Score
=====
* CV: ~0.92, 0.93, 0.91 (for the respective models)
* Public: 0.91503 (138)
* Private: 0.93649 (20)

Congratulations to the winners, this was a really fun contest!

For classification I used a two-stage meta-learner approach with RF, XGBoost and NN as base learners and a combination of those three as meta learners.

The final features I used are as follows:

1. Fraction of auctions won by member (out of total number of auctions member participated in).

2. Mean fraction of bids made by member (in auctions member participated in).

3. Mean fraction of bids made by member within first 10% of bids (in auctions member participated in).

4. Mean fraction of bids made by member within last 25% of bids (in auctions member participated in).

5. Mean fraction of bids made by member (in auctions member won).

6. Mean fraction of bids made by member within first 10% of bids (in auctions member won).

7. Mean fraction of bids made by member within last 25% of bids (in auctions member won).

8. , 9. , 10. Max, Mean and Standard Deviation of number of auctions member is active in per minute.

11., 12., 13. Max, Mean and Standard Deviation of number of auctions member is active in per hour.

14.,15.,16. Probability of bot given Country, Merchandise search category, and device

17., 18., 19. Max, Mean and Standard Deviation of number of ips used per minute.

20., 21., 22. Max, Mean and Standard Deviation of number of ips used per hour.

23. Probabilty of being referred by url : 'vasstdc27m7nks3'

24. Probability of being a Bot given you belong to a cluster of Payment Accounts (Clustered using Hamming Distance, with a Ward Linkage)

25. Probability of being a Bot given you belong to a cluster of Addresses (Clustered using Hamming Distance, with a Ward Linkage)

26., 27., 28.  Mean, Standard Deviation and Sarle's bimodality coefficient of auction bid value (assuming first bid has a value of 10 cents and each subsequent bid increases it by one cent)

29., 30. Mean  and Standard Deviation of length of continuous bid streaks by member.

31., 32. Mean and Standard Deviation of time between Member's bid streaks and previous bidders' bid.

I used a Random Forest classifier with Recursive Feature Elimination + Cross Validation to select these features, my 5-fold cross-validation score was similar to the public LB score with -0.003 difference, and it did not change much on the private LB either. :)

List of decent features according to cross-validation feature selection method I used:
1. absolute number of bids per user.
2. num uniq devices, auctions, ips and urls
3. avg and first-non-zero time diffs between user bids
4. percentage of bids made with favorite(most common) device
5. percentage of bids made from favorite country
6. max number of bids in a single auction
7. number of simultaneous bids
8. number of device switches, numer of auctions switches, number of ips switches, number of countries switches
e.g. this sequence corresponds to 3 'switches' (with 2 devices): [dev1, dev1, dev2, dev2, dev2, dev2, dev1]
9. percentage of user bids made in first/last 5% of auction observed timeline
(observed timeline doesn't correspond to real auction timeline, but in my opinion still helps to understand if user prefers to bid at the beginning or end of an auction)
10. average number of transaction in the same country in 1-hour window with various normalizations
(in my opinion this feature roughly reflects user favorite bidding time of the day)
11. two categ->response transformations features. average response for country and device.
the same idea as discussed here: https://www.kaggle.com/forums/f/15/kaggle-forum/t/8005/replacing-categorical-variables-with-historic-response-rate

there were also a few other trivial features

Congrats to the winners! This was a pretty fun competition; figuring out whether my CV scores were lying or not was interesting to say the least.

I used the following features:

number of bids made at the absolute minimum time delta for each bidder
number of bids made at one single time stamp (essentially counting how many simultaneous bids were made, either in one auction, or across multiple auctions)
number of bids per bidder in each quarter of the auction
number of devices, countries, urls, auctions, ip addresses per bidder 
changes in ip, url, country, device per bidder
average of the time delta between a bidder beating a previous bidder's bid
average time delta between the times each bidder places a bid
Features 1-5 were divided by the total number of bids made by each bidder

I realized my best scores with XGBoost and 10 fold stratified CV 10 times. My best single model score came as a result of using about half the features listed above. I then used the unused features with the first half to create an ensemble which put me near the top 10% on the public leader board. After which I hit a wall and couldn't really see any ways of improving my score. I am very happy to see that my ensemble moved me closer to the top 5% on the private leader board however!

I just want to add one interesting note, K nearest neighbours (using 30 neighbours, and a distance based weight per neighbour) worked surprisingly well with only 5 features. Using the log transformed number of bids, devices, countries, auctions and urls per bidder, I was able to get a score of 0.885 on the public leader board (0.90559 on the private). Not bad for about 15 minutes of effort :)

Even though my final score is dreadful due to starting the competition really late, I can add to this.

Removing the 5 bots that made just one bid increased my CV auc by ~0.03, however adding another step at postprocessing increased the public leaderboard score by about 0.02 aswell.

I searched for the "signatures" of the bids of the five bots that were banned just after one bid in the bid dataset, and it appeared that each one of them had the same signature as a detected bot with a number of bids > 1.
The signatures were the auction, referral site, time, device, ip and (obviously) country of the bids. If there were two bids that had equal information, and one bid belonged to a bot bidder, the other bid was also flagged as a bot bidder.
So I added a post-processing step which searched for all the matches (test set bid - train set flagged bot bid) after classification, and flagged them as a highly probable bot.

Unfortunately, since I had only three submissions to use, I couldn't experiment with searching for matches in the test set as well (same signature as a highly probable bot from the test set), and then copying the probabilities to the max of the two.

The feature calculation consists of two parts one is to determine what is the probability of bots using the "search category" and then to determine what is the weighted probability of a member being a bot based on the number of bids of that member that originated with certain "search categories".

To make it a little clear, I have provided an example below:

u2             No     s1                            10
u2             No     s2                            8
