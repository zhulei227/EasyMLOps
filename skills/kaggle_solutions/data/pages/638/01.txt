NN is not a replacement for Gemma, but it was introduced to exclude word sequences that clearly worsen the score before letting Gemma score them.

While Gemma is a transformer, I chose a ConvNet inspired by SE-ResNet for two reasons: (1) I'm not very experienced with training transformers, and (2) in this competition, how nearby words are arranged seemed to be more important than their global relationships. The model architecture can be found here. (Pretraining wasn’t actually necessary, and online learning during exploration turned out to be sufficient.)

I believe the key point to solving sample5 is block rotation. 😄

A common pattern observed in top solutions is a rotation mechanism that inserts blocks to their near neighbors. In other words, these solutions explore the space through small transformations without significantly altering the current state.

If you think about it, the difficulty in exploration may make solutions scoring 28.52 and 31.5 seem far apart. However, I believe they are actually different local minima within the same basin.

Congratulations on the 1st place and thanks for sharing. I'm curious about how well your model worked.

I tried various methods to prune the solutions before running the gemma score (e.g., smaller gemma model, word scores at a given position + best path til the end based on average word scores, …) with some degree of success. However, none was nearly as effective as a simple NN model. I started with a tiny convolution model (21k parameters) and ended up sticking with it because larger models didn't seem to perform any better and I was able to train it on Kaggle's CPU (allowing me to save the GPU quota). Initially I was using pruning stochastically (to assign a probability of picking a solution), but in light to how well it performed, I switched to using it deterministically (with a parameter that indicated which percentage of steps used the model to prune versus running the gemma scorer directly).

For 1k batches the average ranking error was anywhere from 70 to 90 (out of 1024 positions). Moreover, the top 10% solutions were 60% to 85% correct (i.e., they were also within the top 10% of real scores) and they included the top 10 real solutions. These numbers are for the cases in which the train and test samples were generated from different experiments. If I mixed them, the performance would be much better because of some level of leakage between the train and test samples.

Larger models (including attention models with rotary positional embeddings) didn't perform better, but I wonder if it was because I trained them with a small sample and for few epochs (training on CPU takes a while). I wonder how much better a larger and more sophisticated model would do. How well did you model perform?

Regarding the error values, they depend on how the data for training and inference is sampled, so I don’t think it makes much sense to discuss them in detail. We trained the model as part of the exploration process, and during that process, we printed the Spearman’s correlation. However, the value varied significantly depending on whether it was right after a kick or not.
