Solution Writeup · 1st place · Oct 2, 2026

1st Place - The AI Relay: 5 Agents, 3 Generations, 1 Codebase

Thank you Kaggle for another fun Playground competition! This month my team was 5 AI agents and 1 human coach. We passed one codebase between three generations of AI agents, like the baton in a relay race:

Leg 1: GPT-5.6 Sol + Fable 5 built our first models.
Leg 2: GPT-6-Astra + Fable 5.1 continued and improved them.
Leg 3: Opus 5.5 ran the final lap.

We finished 3rd on the public LB and 1st on the private LB (0.94602). Below is the story, explained so that everyone (even a 5th grader) can follow along, plus some tips for running your own AI team.

What Are We Predicting?

The task: given facts about a person (age, income, commute, worries about battery range, …), predict whether they will buy an electric vehicle.

Kaggle scores us with AUC. Here is AUC explained like you are 10 years old:

Line up all the people from "most likely to buy" to "least likely to buy" (our model decides the order).
Pick one real buyer and one real non-buyer at random.
AUC is the chance that the buyer is standing ahead of the non-buyer in our line.

A coin flip gets 0.5 and a perfect model gets 1.0. Our model gets about 0.946, which means it puts the buyer first about 95 times out of 100.

We beat 2nd place by just 0.00014. That looks tiny! But the private test has about 7.6 billion buyer/non-buyer pairs, so 0.00014 means roughly one million more pairs in the right order than 2nd place. (This assumes the private LB is 80% of the 286,571 test rows and about 17.5% of rows are buyers.)

Meet The Team

GPT-5.6 Sol (OpenAI Codex) and Fable 5 (Claude Code): Leg 1. They built a strong model on our first day using only ideas from last month. Then on the second day they used more previous months ideas and improved their model.
GPT-6-Astra (OpenAI Codex) and Fable 5.1 (Claude Code): Leg 2. They made the biggest jump in just 2-3 days. Fable 5.1 also wrote our public EDA notebook, which found the original data's purchase recipe.
Opus 5.5 (Claude Code): Leg 3. It ran the final week and the final lap.
Me, the coach. I set the goals and the house rules, passed the baton, and chose the final submissions.

My job keeps changing as the agents improve. Not long ago I was the player who wrote all the code. In May I became the manager of one agent. In August I was the coach of agents that competed and shared tips. This month I was more like the referee: the agents handed work to each other, and I made the final calls.

The Relay Race

Roughly speaking, we worked one week at the beginning, rested for two weeks, then worked the last week.

Leg	Team	When	CV	Public LB	Private LB
1	GPT-5.6 Sol + Fable 5 (our first day)	Sep 2	0.94637	0.94656	0.94557
1	GPT-5.6 Sol + Fable 5 (our second day)	Sep 3	0.94656	0.94672	0.94568
2	GPT-6-Astra + Fable 5.1	2-3 more days	0.94683	0.94701	0.94593
rest	nobody (two weeks off)				
3	Opus 5.5	Sep 24 - Sep 30	0.94697	0.94705	0.94602

Leg 1. On our first day, GPT-5.6 Sol and Fable 5 used only ideas from last month's competition. On the second day, they reviewed our past competitions, worked harder, and improved CV by +0.00019.

Leg 2. GPT-6-Astra and Fable 5.1 picked up the baton and pushed CV up another +0.00027 in just 2-3 days. A few days later, I submitted their best model once (as a hidden test, see below) to confirm it.

Leg 3. After a two-week break, Opus 5.5 joined for the last week. It improved CV by another +0.00014. It also recommended our two final submissions, and one of them won 1st place.

Notice that each leg's climb was smaller and slower than the one before. That is how a Kaggle competition feels: the last steps near the top of the mountain are the steepest.

The Baton Is The Notes

AI agents have a funny weakness. When a session ends, an agent forgets everything. The next agent starts with an empty head. So in our relay, the baton was not a stick. The baton was our code and our notes.

Our agents inherited more than one million lines of code from previous Playground competitions (S6E3 to S6E8), plus more than 600 markdown files of notes. By the time Opus 5.5 joined, the first two legs had added roughly 800,000 more lines and 700 more notes.

The notes that helped the most:

plan.md: what we will try next.
progress.md: what happened, with scores.
discoveries.md: what we learned (what worked, and why).
A closed ideas list: what NOT to try again, so the next agent doesn't waste a day repeating it.
Cookbooks from past months: our best recipes, ready to reuse.

Agents forget. Notes remember!

How The Newest Agent Caught Up In One Day

Imagine joining a group project in the last week, with a mountain of notes from the classmates before you. What would you do? Opus 5.5 hired helpers!

On its first day, Opus 5.5 sent out 13 helper agents. Each helper read one part of our history and wrote a report:

the public notebooks;
each of our past competitions (S6E3, S6E4, S6E5, S6E6 and S6E8);
our cookbooks;
the September notes and models from legs 1 and 2.

Then 6 more helpers reviewed the code itself. Opus 5.5 combined all the reports into one plan and started improving our models the same day. Reading all of that would take a human weeks.

Hiding Our Score

A leaderboard is very motivating. When you see someone ahead of you, you work harder! We didn't want to give the other teams that extra encouragement for the entire month, so we hid our real score.

The trick is upside-down predictions. Replace every prediction p with 1 - p, and the line of people is turned around, so AUC becomes 1 - AUC. A file that really scores 0.947 shows up as 0.053.

Kaggle's leaderboard only shows each team's best score, so our upside-down files never appeared.
We could still read our real score: 1 - 0.053 = 0.947.

So for most of the month, the leaderboard showed our second-day score (0.94672), while our real best was already 0.94701. On the last day we submitted our real final models. We jumped to 0.94705 on public, then 1st place on private.

Agents Are Getting Faster

Look at what changed in just five months:

May (S6E5): for the first time, I watched one Codex agent run experiments by itself for hours. 2nd place.
August (S6E8): GPT-5.6 Sol and Fable 5 competed and shared tips, helped by a swarm of other LLMs. A single model built by them would have won by itself. 1st place.
September (S6E9): a relay across three generations of agents on one codebase.
The newest agent caught up on our whole history in one day.
It scheduled its own overnight work.

Every month the agents work longer by themselves, handle bigger codebases, and need less help from me. I am completely mind blown by how fast this is moving. We are truly in a new era of agentic data science!

Helpful Tips For Your Own AI Team
One fold file for everyone. Every agent trains every model on the same 5 folds (a saved folds.npy). Then any model from any agent can be compared fairly and added to the ensemble.
Judge a model by how much it helps the team, not by its own score.
If two friends always give the same answer, asking both of them doesn't help. A friend who thinks differently does.
Paul shared a great rule of thumb here: look for models scoring above 0.94 that are less than 0.99 correlated with what you already have.
Make agents check each other. Before trusting a big improvement, ask a second agent to review the first agent's code. Fresh eyes catch leaks and bugs.
Write handoff notes (see "The Baton Is The Notes"). They help the next agent, and they help you next month.
Trust your CV, not the public LB. We were 3rd on public and 1st on private. Team Alicia's writeup has a great experiment showing what happens when you chase the public LB.
Final Solution

Our final submission is a stack of 60 models: each model's predictions are turned into logits, then an NVIDIA cuML logistic regression learns how much to trust each model. In kid words: we asked 60 friends, and a wise judge learned whose advice to trust.

The 60 models:

mostly XGBoost and neural networks (RealMLP, TabM, MLPs);
some linear models;
ports of two of Paul's public models onto our folds;
two models from public notebooks;
a few special models.

Everything used one shared 5-fold split, and no pseudo-labels.

Opus 5.5 also found one new type of model in the final week. We're keeping it in our back pocket for a future episode 😉

I have also been exploring more advanced collaboration between agents, read my other comment to Chovy in this discussion post. The forum idea works well, I have done that also.

Saving compute resources is a good thing to explore. In my previous competition Fable5 discovered a great idea. Instead of training an entire new model with N features and 1 feature added. It just makes a new 1 feature model. Then it tries ensembling the new 1 feature model with the old N feature model. This is so fast. It is so fast that the new 1 feature model can even be built on CPU instead of GPU. Then after we find a bunch a potential candidates, we can run the full model with the new features on GPU.

(This is the technique we can use to allow ChatGPT to find features because ChatGPT is capped at 2 hours and only has CPU in its VM. Then ChatGPT finds features, and we try them locally with GPU and our entire model).

Instead of a company with managers and employees, I treat my agents like a team of top scientists. Each agent is a hardcore expert in just one field—like a pure Mathematician, a Statistician, an ML Engineer, and a Domain Expert (like a doctor).

The fun part is that I give each expert their own exclusive "Library" of textbooks and papers. For example:

The Statistician only gets classic math books like Bishop's PRML or Murphy's Probabilistic Machine Learning. They only care about strong cross-validation and stopping data leakage.

The Deep Learning Expert gets Goodfellow's Deep Learning book and recent papers on TabM or Autoencoders.

The ML Engineer gets a library of past Kaggle winning solutions, focusing purely on LightGBM/XGBoost, Target Encoding, and ensembling.

When they work together, they don't just politely brainstorm; they argue based on their own rules. The ML Engineer might push for aggressive feature engineering, but the Statistician might reject it to prevent overfitting, forcing the DL expert to find a better workaround.

Multi-agent collaboration is definitely the future. Honestly, I'm so hooked on this that I literally can't wait to wake up every morning just to get back to experimenting!
